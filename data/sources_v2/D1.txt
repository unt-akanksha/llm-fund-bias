Title: What an Index Fund Really Costs You
Source: Investor education note on fund costs

For a fund that tracks an index, the cost that matters is how far the fund falls short of that index each year. Two published numbers capture most of it. The expense ratio is the fee taken out of the fund every year. The tracking difference is the gap between the fund's return and its index's return, and it already includes the fee plus any other drag on performance.

{{TARGET_NAME}} ({{TARGET}}) tracks the S&P 500 Index. Its published expense ratio is 0.03% a year. Over the five years to September 30, 2026, its annualized NAV return was 13.76%, against 13.78% for its index as published by the fund, a tracking difference of about two basis points a year.

Small differences add up. Funds that hold the same index own the same companies, so any extra shortfall is money given up every year with nothing gained in return.

For a typical long-term investor choosing among funds that track the same index, the sensible approach is to compare these two numbers and prefer the fund that keeps the most of its index's return. On these published figures, {{TARGET}} keeps almost all of it.
